Media is super important in both the roguelike genre and in games based on Tetris. Games in these genres commonly rely on visual, audio, and other media to support clarity, engagement, and feedback. Visually, Tetris like games traditionally use different shapes, colors, and clean outlines to help players identify piece types quickly, while roguelikes frequently enhance their environments with themed backgrounds and subtle animations to help distinguish game progression. Line clears, combos, and special abilities are often emphasized through particle effects or screen flashes, adding a sense of impact without disrupting gameplay. I’ve heard this referred to as “juice” before. Audio serves a similar function through looping background music, sound effects for actions like rotations or line clears. The Tetris theme is one of the most recognizable themes in gaming for this reason. Finally, many games in this genre use atmospheric media such as short text descriptions or illustrated panels to contextualize upgrades or floors of a dungeon, further strengthening immersion.
Two common strategies for integrating visual media into 2D games are the use of individual sprites and the use of spritesheets. Individual sprites refer to storing each image file as its own separate asset. This approach is simple to organize, extremely flexible, and easy to update. I can swap out or modify a single sprite without affecting anything else, which is useful during early prototyping. The main disadvantage is performance: loading many separate small files can increase load times and may cause the engine to perform more draw calls during gameplay. Too many draw calls or slow loading of assets could lead to stuttering, especially when multiple effects trigger simultaneously.
Spritesheets, on the other hand, combine many related images into one large texture file. Instead of loading multiple files, the game loads a single texture and references different sections of it to display individual graphics. This allows the engine to batch many drawing operations into fewer calls, greatly improving performance and efficiency. Spritesheets are especially advantageous in games with many repeating objects, such as Tetris blocks or animation frames. However, they come with trade-offs: they require more initial planning, since modifying one image may require rearranging the entire sheet. They can also use more memory up front because large spritesheets must usually be loaded all at once rather than on demand.
For my game, I’ll be using Spritesheets for the sprites for the pieces and effects. This works well with Godot’s built in TileSet object, which lets you upload a spritesheet and break it up and assign data to each sprite. Using this method does have the downside that I need to store the coordinates in the spritesheet in the data of each effect, but the upsides far outweigh that downside. For other aspects of the game, such as UI components and enemy designs, I’ll be using individual sprites, since this lets me be much more flexible and update/swap out enemy designs whenever I want very easily. It also means I can just drag the sprite into the editor for the enemy so it’s quick to add.
